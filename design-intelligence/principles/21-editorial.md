# Editorial Design

## What it is
Content-forward interfaces (publications, long-form writing, documentation) where sustained reading
quality is the primary success metric — motion and interaction are subordinate to, not competing with,
the reading experience.

## Core pattern from this research
By-Kin's "weighted," unobtrusive scroll and restrained transitions (`research/awwwards/10-by-kin.md`
§8): motion tuned specifically to support reading rhythm rather than draw attention to itself. Mat
Voyce's principle that a site's form should echo its subject's specialty (`research/awwwards/11-mat-
voyce.md` §13) also applies here — an editorial site's typography should be the primary craft
statement.

## Implementation guidance
- Line length, line-height, and paragraph width (`05-typography.md`) matter more here than almost any
  other surface type — get these numerically right, don't eyeball them.
- Any transition/scroll effect should be evaluated by whether it helps or interrupts reading momentum —
  if a reader would need to pause and reorient after a transition, it's too aggressive for this context.
- Use a genuine typographic hierarchy (not just size) to distinguish headline, deck/subhead, byline,
  body, and captions — editorial credibility is carried substantially by typographic discipline.
- Pull quotes, drop caps, and other editorial devices should be used sparingly and consistently, not
  scattered arbitrarily for visual interest.

## Failure mode
Applying marketing-site conventions (large CTAs, card grids, heavy motion) to content whose actual job
is sustained reading — undermines the exact quality (focus, trust in the text) editorial content depends
on.

## When to allow more visual expression
Feature/long-form storytelling pieces (as opposed to standard articles) can justify more scroll-driven,
immersive treatment specifically when the story's structure supports it — but the "does this help or
interrupt reading" test still applies at every step.
