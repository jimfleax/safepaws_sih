# Common Principles Across All 25 References

Patterns that recur across references with genuinely independent evidence (different studios,
different countries, different eras) rather than a single studio's habit. Each links back to source
dossiers so the claim is traceable.

## 1. Every awarded site does *something* deliberately, not everything competently
No reference in this set is "good at all 14 quality dimensions equally." Stripe (01) is weak on
minimalism but excellent at segmented proof. Bruno Simon (07) is weak on conventional accessibility
but exceptional at concept commitment. Obys (05) and Resn (06) are recognized for consistency across
many projects, not for one universally-optimized site. The pattern: pick what this specific brief
needs to be excellent at, and be honestly mediocre-but-acceptable elsewhere, rather than diffusing
effort across every dimension. See `principles/00-master-principles.md`.

## 2. Restraint and richness are both earned, not defaulted
Lusion (08) explicitly rejected minimalism for their own site because their brief needed to demonstrate
range. By-Kin (10), Aesop (14), Kinfolk (24), and Muji (25) are restrained because restraint is their
actual brand argument. Neither choice is "more sophisticated" — both dossiers independently show the
choice being justified by the brief, not applied by default. This is the strongest, most repeated
finding in the set and the core argument against a "use minimal design" or "add motion" universal rule.

## 3. Motion/interaction devices map to a real concept, not to "engagement"
Bruno Simon's antenna physics (07), Iventions' spotlit-installation metaphor (21), Zajno's published
scroll/3D coupling (09), and Spotify Wrapped Party's live-multiplayer visuals (17) are all named,
specific techniques tied to a specific idea the project needed to express. None of the strongest
references in this set use motion because "motion looks premium." See `motion/principles.md`.

## 4. Navigation/IA choices encode business model or content structure, not convention
Obys's two-item nav (05) matches a boutique, few-clients model. Framer's role-based nav (02) matches a
heterogeneous audience. Mat Voyce's three-mode IA (11) matches genuinely different content types.
Stripe's audience-segmented sections (01) match distinct buyer personas. The pattern: IA should be
derived from something true about the product/audience, not copied from a competitor's structure.

## 5. Award-winning is not synonymous with flawless, and is never synonymous with commercially
successful
Uncommon Studio (04) ships `user-scalable=no`, a real accessibility failure, despite genuine craft
elsewhere. Cowboy (13) won real design awards and still went bankrupt. This system treats award
recognition as evidence of craft in specific dimensions the awarding body scores, not as a certificate
of overall quality or business viability. See `anti-generic-ai/` for why this distinction matters for
an AI agent specifically.

## 6. Technical transparency (when disclosed) correlates with reproducible craft
The clearest, most actionable dossiers in this set (Zajno 09, Bruno Simon 07) are the ones where the
practitioners themselves published their actual technique. This is also a research-methodology finding:
this system's own confidence tiers are higher for entries with named, checkable technical disclosure.

## 7. A distinguishing visual/technical device is usually singular, not compound
Stripe's italic-headline device, Resn's "gooey feeling over fixed style," Cowboy's pure-achromatic
chrome, Zajno's scroll+3D coupling — in each case, one specific device carries the differentiation,
supported by otherwise unremarkable, competent execution elsewhere. Sites that try to be distinctive in
five ways simultaneously are not represented among the strongest entries in this set.
